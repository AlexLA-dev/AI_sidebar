Why the four-day week works — and when it doesn't

A year after 61 companies cut the working week to four days, the results are in. Revenue held steady, burnout fell sharply, and most firms never went back.

By Elena Marsh · 9 min read

When 61 companies agreed to try a four-day week for six months, many expected chaos: missed deadlines, angry clients and exhausted managers covering the gaps. Twelve months later, 56 of them are still working four days, and 18 have made the change permanent in employee contracts.

The trial asked firms to cut hours by 20% without cutting pay, on the condition that output stayed the same. Researchers tracked revenue, absenteeism, staff turnover and self-reported wellbeing before, during and after the pilot.

What changed

Revenue across participating companies rose by 1.4% on average during the trial, roughly in line with previous years. The bigger shifts were human: 71% of employees reported lower levels of burnout, sick days fell by about two thirds, and the number of people leaving dropped by 57% compared with the same period a year earlier.

Managers credited three habits for the results: fewer and shorter meetings, protected "focus blocks" with notifications switched off, and clearer priorities agreed at the start of each week. "We didn't work faster," one operations director said. "We stopped doing things that didn't matter."

"The day off is the headline, but the real change was deciding, every week, what not to do."

Where it struggled

Not every team adapted. Customer-facing roles with fixed opening hours needed staggered schedules, and several small firms said covering holidays became harder. Two companies in the trial reverted to five days after the six months, citing client expectations and a busy season.

Researchers also warn that the results come from volunteers: companies that chose to take part were likely more prepared for the change than average. Sectors such as healthcare and manufacturing were under-represented.

What it means for your team

The authors suggest starting with a time-boxed pilot of three to six months, agreeing in advance which metrics define success, and redesigning work before removing hours — not after. Teams that simply squeezed five days into four reported more stress, not less.

For small teams, the advice is to cover customer touchpoints with rotating days off rather than closing on Fridays. And for managers, the clearest lesson may be the simplest: measure outcomes, not hours at the desk.
