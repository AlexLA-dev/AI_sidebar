Why we forget most of what we read

In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus spent months learning lists of nonsense syllables, like “WID” and “ZOF”, and testing himself on them later. Forgetting, he found, is fastest at the start: much of a list was gone within the first hour, and more by the next day. After that the loss slowed down.

His curve has a catch. Nonsense syllables mean nothing, while what matters to us, or connects to what we already know, is easier to keep. Still, the shape holds up: without a second look, most of a long article fades within days.

Two habits help. Coming back to something after a gap, a day later and then a week later, keeps more than reading it twice in a row. And putting an idea into your own words, even one sentence, makes it stick better than rereading.

Things to ask

  • “What's the main idea, in one sentence?”
  • Select “His curve has a catch” and ask: “Is that true?”
  • “What did later research find about this?”
